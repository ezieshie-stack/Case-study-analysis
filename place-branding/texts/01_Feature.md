# Text 1: Feature story

**Audience:** International students and other newcomers to Örebro
**Format:** Feature story, approx. 800 to 1,200 words
**Intended platform:** The Local Sweden, the English-language news site for international residents of Sweden

> ✎ Build the feature around a person, an experience or a question. Everything below must come from your reporting: notes with times, quotations from the verification log. No invented detail, no composite characters.
>
> ✎ **Your voice, as the lecturer asked.** You are the narrator. You set the scene, explain, connect, and decide what matters. The interviewees supply quotations, which are short and chosen for colour or emotion, not for facts. Rough guide: no more than about a quarter of the feature should be inside quotation marks. Never string quotes together ("[owner's name] said... She said... She added...").
> ✎ Paraphrase facts in your own words and attribute them: *[owner's name] and her husband opened the doors on 1 October.* Save direct quotation for what only she could say, in the way only she would say it.
> ✎ Use "I" sparingly, if at all, and only where your presence adds something, for example as a newcomer yourself. Your voice shows mainly in what you notice and how you frame it.
> ✎ Illustration of the difference, with invented content, not for use: *Transcript style:* "[owner's name] said it was hard. She said many people came. She said they were happy." *Narrated style:* "By the second week, the question at the door had changed from 'When do you open?' to 'Is the corner table free?'" The second one is the writer's voice; a single quotation would then add [owner's name]'s.
> ✎ Lens reminder (lecturer, 15 Oct): treat "the place of opportunities" as a proposition to investigate, and say plainly that one restaurant cannot represent the whole city. Build the opening scene from your observations, and connect the restaurant to its neighbourhood.
> ✎ Earlier note: Weave in one or two facts from `10_Orebro_Research_Notes.md` in your own words, and let the family's experience test the promise.

### [[Optional standfirst: one sentence that frames the angle]]

[[LEAD. Open in a scene from your observation notes or the interview setting: a time, a person doing something, one sensory detail. Two to four sentences. Do not open with the name of the place or a definition of it.]]

> ✎ Friedmann: write for the eye and ear. Smith: an engaging lead earns the reader's next paragraph; it does not summarise.

[[NUT GRAPH. One paragraph. What this story is about, why it matters to this audience now, and the question it answers. Name the place here if the lead did not.]]

> ✎ The nut graph is the contract with the reader. It should connect the scene above to the broader point: what newcomers (or whoever your audience is) can learn from this person's experience of the place.

[[BODY 1: The person and their connection to the place. Who they are, how they came to it, in their words where possible. First quotation here.]]

[[BODY 2: What makes the place special to them, shown through one or two specific memories or details. Quotation.]]

[[BODY 3: Useful background from desk research: what the place is, who runs it, a key date or fact, with a source in the log. Keep it to one paragraph so the story stays with people.]]

[[BODY 4: What a newcomer should know, in practical terms, drawn from the interview and your own visit.]]

[[BODY 5: A different perspective. A second voice (visitor, participant) or your own observation that complicates or adds to the main interviewee's view. This is where "what could be better" belongs.]]

> ✎ The brief: "recognising that other people may experience it differently." One honest paragraph here is worth more than three enthusiastic ones.

[[ENDING. Return to a detail from the lead, or to a final line from the interviewee, or leave the reader with a clear point to consider. Do not summarise. Do not end on a slogan.]]
